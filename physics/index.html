01 The world

An Earth-like world, by the numbers

The Reach orbits Alpha Centauri A, a star a little heavier than the Sun, from a little farther out than Earth sits from ours. Its pull is Earth's own, and its air rests on the same standard atmosphere pilots fly by on Earth.

Heights are in feet above MSL: Mean Standard Level, the surveyed surface on which that standard atmosphere is defined. Pressure keeps rising below it, so a basin can sit below zero.

The day is 25.4 hours long. The war keeps Earth's 24-hour clock anyway, on a world that does not keep it, and nobody aboard is going to fix that.

What the air is made of

02 Gravity

One gravity, for everything

One gravity runs the whole game, 9.81 metres per second squared: the DART-20's rounds drop by it, and the fifty-ton machine you are sitting in falls by it. It is Earth's gravity because your body already knows it. Anything lighter only feels floaty, and nobody can tell you why.

So a fall is a real fall. Arrive at 6 m/s or slower, a drop of about 6 feet, and the legs take it. Every metre per second past that costs each leg 14 of its 150, so an unpowered drop from 47 feet takes both legs off.

Arrival speed = √(2 × g × height)

Each leg takes (arrival speed − 6 m/s) × 14

03 Flight

Jump jets: a climb you pay for

The jets push the VIGIL up at 14 m/s, about 2,760 feet a minute, and they own the whole flight. Once you leave the ground you keep the heading and speed you had at liftoff, and the legs cannot steer you. Run and jump, and you arc up and forward. The torso still turns, so you can fly over a fight and shoot down into its back.

A flight-control governor caps the climb at 150 feet. Over the last 40 feet it bleeds the thrust away, so the VIGIL settles at its ceiling instead of striking it. Holding there hovers, and still burns.

Coming down, an auto-flare lights the jets at 16 feet and brakes you to a 4 m/s touchdown. It needs fuel and a reactor that is not overheated. Fly hot, or fly the tank dry, and you arrive at whatever speed gravity gave you.

04 Weight

Every pound costs you

An empty VIGIL weighs 76,000 lb. Loaded to its maximum operating weight it is 106,240 lb, and you can load it past that. Weight is not a number on a card: it reaches into everything the machine does.

  • Speed. Top speed slides from 45 mph empty to 39 at maximum weight. Past it, each extra percent of payload costs another 0.28 mph.
  • Cooling. A heavier machine sheds heat worse, and the loss grows with the square of the load: 70% of its cooling with the starter kit, 57% at maximum weight.
  • Flight. The jets lift what you weigh against the empty machine. Heavier burns fuel and heat faster and tops out lower: 102 ft for the starter kit with jets fitted.
  • Inertia. From a standstill it takes about two and a half seconds to reach full speed, and a second and a half to stop.

05 Ground and water

Slow, slip, stall

Hills slow you the way they slow anything heavy: every step lifts the whole machine. Gentle ground costs little and steep ground costs a lot, and load makes it worse. Each band warns you of the next.

  • Slow. Below 45°, speed falls with the slope. Steeper is slower, and heavier is slower still.
  • Slip. From 45° the feet lose their grip. Push on and you slide back at 2.5 m/s. Let go and you stop.
  • Stall. At 65° the legs cannot reach the next step. It is a wall, and weight does not move it.

Speed on a slope = flat speed ÷ (1 + k × sin(slope)2.5)

k = 3.5 + 2.3 × the share of payload carried

Wading

Water drags on the legs, and drag climbs with the cube of your speed, so the deeper it is the slower you wade: about 16 mph a metre deep, 13 at two metres, 10 at four, and 8 at nine, waist deep. Go wholly under for ten seconds and the VIGIL is lost.

Wading speed = ∛(400 ÷ depth in metres) m/s

06 Collisions

Fifty tons, moving

Mechs collide like the heavy machines they are, and the server settles every collision the same way for every pilot. A ram's damage comes from the energy of the collision itself: the closing speed squared, times the two machines' reduced mass. Both mechs take it, and the lighter one takes more.

Reduced mass = m₁ × m₂ ÷ (m₁ + m₂)

Ram damage ∝ reduced mass × closing speed², split by the other mech's share of the weight

  • A shove is a shove. Below 3 m/s of closing speed, about 7 mph, nothing is damaged.
  • Behind is worse. A ram from behind lands on the rear plate, 150 points against the front's 200.
  • Momentum knocks you down. Measured along the line of contact: if the other mech's is half again yours, you go over. A mech standing still has none, so any real charge puts it down.
  • Getting up. Two seconds down and standing, then two seconds when nothing can knock you over again.
  • Teammates push. They never damage you and never knock you down.

07 Ballistics

Rounds that fall, and drift

The DART-20 is the long gun, and its rounds obey gravity. They leave the muzzle at 330 m/s and are zeroed at 300 metres, 328 yards. Past that they drop below your line of sight, so at range you hold over, walking the target up the scope's ladder: about two ticks at 1,000 yards.

Drop = ½ × g × R × (R − Z) ÷ v²

R the range, Z the zero, v the muzzle velocity

Crosswind pushes every round that spends time in the air. It turns a fast round by a fixed angle, so the miss grows with range, and a lobbed shell drifts for its whole flight. A headwind or a tailwind does nothing sideways.

The AI pilots fight under the same physics. Their rounds drop and drift like yours, and they hold over by the same numbers. When they miss, it is their aim, never different rules.

08 Weather

Weather that moves

Weather on the war map is not rolled grid by grid. Four pressure systems at a time cross the map from west to east, each about 240 km across, so one storm lies over five or six grids at once and takes five days to cross. The map is about 650 by 500 km, its grids 50 km apart.

  • Pressure. A low drops the altimeter by 0.30 to 0.90 inHg. A high raises it by 0.15 to 0.45.
  • Wind. It turns counter-clockwise round a low and clockwise round a high, strongest on the slope between, up to 26 mph on top of a steady 6 mph westerly.
  • Temperature. It follows the season and the hour. A passing low cools the air by up to 14 °F and damps the swing between day and night, the way cloud does.
  • Rain, snow, fog. Rain or snow is the temperature's call. Fog forms in calm air in the early morning and burns off by nine.
  • One sky. The systems are worked out from the clock every machine shares, so every pilot sees the same weather, and a battle on the Front flies its grid's.

09 Sound

Seen first, heard later

Sound crosses the air at about 343 m/s, faster when it is warm and slower when it is cold. So a gun 600 metres away is seen first and heard almost two seconds later.

The air also soaks up the high notes over distance, more in dry or cold air, so a far shot arrives as a dull thud rather than a crack. A hot dry desert and a cold wet storm sound different at range, because they are.

Speed of sound = 331.3 × √(1 + T ÷ 273.15) m/s

What the air takes ISO 9613-1, from frequency, temperature and humidity

10 Thin air Modelled now · coming to the cockpit

Hot and high

Every grid on the war map already knows its air: its pressure, its temperature, and from those its density altitude, worked out the way pilots work it out. Density altitude is how high the air behaves, whatever height you are standing at.

When it reaches the cockpit, thin air will cut the jets' thrust, make cooling worse and lower the weight you can carry. The machine that is a rocket on a cold morning low in a valley comes up short on a hot afternoon up high, so the best loadout depends on where you are dropping this week.

11 The crossing

Thirty-two years at 0.130 c

The Archive left Earth on a burn at one gravity for 46 days, which took it to 0.130 of the speed of light: about 39,000 km a second, Earth to the Moon in ten seconds. Then its engines went quiet. The coast lasts 32 and a half years, and a second burn at one gravity, 46 days again, brings it into orbit over The Reach.

  • Why 46 days. At one gravity, reaching 0.130 c takes 46.3 days of burn by relativity's own arithmetic.
  • Slower clocks. At that speed time aboard runs 0.85% slower than on Earth. Every date the Archive keeps is Earth's.
  • Speeds do not simply add. The faster ship that left the Archive flew at 0.25 c by Earth's reckoning. Launched from a ship already doing 0.130 c, it needed 0.124 c more, not 0.120.

Burn time = (c ÷ g) × artanh(v ÷ c)

Relative speed = (u − v) ÷ (1 − uv ÷ c²)
